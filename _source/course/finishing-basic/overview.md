## What this module is for

This is the end of the level. It holds one lesson, {{topic:basiccovered}}, which says what you can now
do, compresses the level into seven habits, and names the module to come back to when one of them
slips.

It teaches nothing new. And it's written for the reader who stops here, which is most readers.
Using Copilot well is a complete skill on its own. Building agents is a different job, for people
whose work is building them.

## Before you start

The rest of the level. The lesson refers back to every module, and the self-check below draws on all
of them. If a question stumps you, reread the topic its answer names. That's what it's for.

Allow about 15 minutes.

## What you will be able to do

By the end of this module you should be able to:

- name the habits that make Copilot worth using, and the module behind each;
- say which lesson to reread for a problem you meet next month;
- explain what this level does not prepare you for;
- decide, without pressure either way, whether to go on to building agents.

## The thread through this module

Every example comes from Technik, the fictional manufacturer this course is built around. See the
[scenario](../scenario/index.html) for the company and its documents.

The lesson walks through one week of work: a QN summary, a question to Chat about weld prep inspection,
a table of supplier certificates, a note about overdue documents, and an agent answering outside its
job. Each step is one habit from the level, and none of them builds anything.

## If you stop here

You've finished. Nothing later in the course is needed to use Copilot well, and nothing here gets
less useful because you didn't go on. Keep the lesson as the page to come back to.

If you do want to build agents, the course continues for engineers, and the place to start is the
roadmap's link at the foot of the page.

## Self-check

These cover the whole level. Each answer explains the reasoning, not just the verdict.

<details>
<summary>1. A month from now, Copilot gives you a fluent answer about a Technik procedure with no citation. What's the first thing you do?</summary>

Treat it as a guess until you know where it came from. A fluent answer with nothing to open is either
from the web, from the model's training, or from a search that found nothing useful. Ask again naming
the document, or attach it, and then open the citation. Reread {{topic:hallucination}}, and
{{topic:m365chat}} for telling which Copilot you have.
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Your Microsoft 365 Copilot licence arrives. Which two lessons are worth rereading that day, and why?</summary>

{{topic:tenantgrounding}} and {{topic:missingcontent}}. Overnight, Copilot starts searching your
documents, mail and chats, so its answers about work change, and so do its mistakes: a real sentence
from the wrong document, or a file it should have found and didn't. Those two lessons explain both.
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. A colleague says Copilot is useless because it never finds the right procedure. What would you ask them before agreeing?</summary>

Which Copilot they have, how they asked, and where the procedure lives. Without the licence, Chat
doesn't search work documents at all. With it, the usual causes are permissions, location, format and
age, not the model. And a vague question gets a vague search. Reread {{topic:missingcontent}} and
{{topic:clarity}}.
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. You've used Copilot daily for three months and it's been right almost every time. Can you stop checking?</summary>

Not for anything that leaves your hands. Being right often is exactly what makes the occasional wrong
answer dangerous, because it sounds the same as the right ones. Keep checking what you send, sign or act
on: a drafted reply, a recap's action items, a number in a table. Reread {{topic:rai}}.
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Your manager asks whether finishing this level means you can build the team an agent. What's the honest answer?</summary>

No. This level teaches using Copilot and the agents other people built. Building one means writing
its instructions, choosing its knowledge, testing it and publishing it, and that's a separate course
for engineers. What you can do is be a good user of the agent someone builds: one who reads its
citations and reports its mistakes. Reread {{topic:basiccovered}}.
</details>
